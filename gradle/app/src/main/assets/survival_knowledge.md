# Apocalyptic AI — Offline Survival Knowledge Library

This offline library is available on the device after installation. It combines original, safety-reviewed field notes with public-domain outdoor books included in `library/`. It is reference material, not a substitute for local emergency services, trained medical care, or current official guidance.

## How to use this library

When conditions are dangerous, use the order: **awareness, communication, shelter, water, first aid, warmth, food, navigation, and repair**. Work in pairs when possible. Tell someone your route. Keep a written plan. Avoid unnecessary travel, unknown water, unstable buildings, wildfire smoke, flooding, contaminated areas, and conflict.

## 1. Immediate priorities

1. Check for immediate hazards: fire, gas, live wires, floodwater, unstable roofs, falling trees, extreme heat or cold, and violence.
2. Move to a safer location only when the move reduces danger.
3. Account for people, children, older adults, and animals. Treat shock and exposure early.
4. Signal for help with voice, whistle, flashlight, mirror, bright cloth, or repeated ground-to-air marks. Use three repeated signals as a common distress pattern, but follow local emergency guidance.
5. Conserve battery. Write down time, direction, landmarks, injuries, medicines, and water status.

## 2. Shelter and exposure

A good shelter is dry, stable, insulated from the ground, protected from wind and precipitation, and ventilated. Avoid dry stream beds, avalanche paths, dead trees, cliffs, damaged structures, and low areas that collect cold air or floodwater.

Use a ground layer first: a sleeping pad, folded clothing, cardboard, dry leaves inside a barrier, or another insulating layer. Add a roof or windbreak. Keep clothing dry. Vent any stove or flame to the outside; carbon monoxide is invisible and odorless. Never sleep beside an unvented combustion device.

In cold conditions, stay dry, block wind, insulate the head and torso, and use layers that can be adjusted before sweating. In heat, rest during the hottest period, cover skin from sun, and drink safely treated water. Confusion, clumsiness, unusual sleepiness, and inability to shiver are warning signs requiring urgent help.

## 3. Water collection and treatment

Prefer sealed water, then a known safe source. Avoid water contaminated by fuel, industrial chemicals, sewage, dead animals, or floodwater. A clear-looking source can still contain pathogens.

Pre-filter muddy water through clean cloth if needed. Use a validated filter according to its instructions, then use a treatment method appropriate for the pathogen risk. Boiling is effective when water reaches a full boil; keep it boiling for the time recommended by current local health guidance, then cool it in a covered container. Chemical treatment must follow the product label exactly. Do not mix chemicals.

Store treated water in clean, covered containers. Keep untreated and treated containers separate. Use clean hands and a clean scoop. Plan a daily minimum and reserve some water for first aid and cooking.

## 4. Fire and heat

Fire is useful for warmth, cooking, and morale, but it causes burns and carbon-monoxide deaths. Select a clear site away from tents, dry grass, overhanging branches, fuel, and structures. Keep water, sand, or a way to smother the fire nearby. Put it completely out before leaving or sleeping.

Dry tinder catches first, then pencil-sized kindling, then larger fuel. Split or shave wet wood to find dry interior fibers. A small controlled fire is easier to manage than a large one. Never use gasoline, solvents, aerosol cans, or unknown chemicals to start a fire.

## 5. First aid and medical planning

Call local emergency services whenever possible. Wear gloves or use a barrier. Check responsiveness, breathing, and major bleeding. For life-threatening bleeding, use firm direct pressure with clean material and keep pressure in place. Add material over the first layer if it soaks through. Do not remove an embedded object.

For burns, stop the burning process and cool with clean, cool running water when available. Do not apply grease, ice directly, or unknown substances. Cover loosely with a clean non-stick dressing and seek medical care.

For suspected fractures, keep the person still, support the injured area, and do not force it straight. For heat illness, move to shade, cool the body, and get urgent help for confusion or collapse. For hypothermia, move to shelter, replace wet layers, insulate, and warm gradually.

Keep a medical card with allergies, medicines, conditions, emergency contacts, and blood type only if verified. Rotate supplies and learn first aid before an emergency.

## 6. Food safety

Eat sealed, shelf-stable foods first. Separate raw food from ready-to-eat food. Wash hands and tools. Discard food that is swollen, leaking, moldy, slimy, foul-smelling, or kept at unsafe temperatures. Do not taste food to test it.

Never eat an unknown plant, mushroom, berry, seed, or animal. Identification must be certain and appropriate to the region and season. Cooking does not neutralize every toxin. Use local extension or health guidance for foraging decisions.

Preserve food only with a tested method. Improvised canning can create botulism risk. Keep food away from rodents, insects, pets, and chemicals.

## 7. Navigation and getting lost

Before travel, record your destination, route, companions, departure time, expected return, and a turnaround time. Carry a paper map, compass, light, water, insulation, whistle, and a way to signal.

If lost, stop moving once you recognize you are uncertain. Breathe, shelter, treat injuries, assess water, and mark your location. Use the map and compass only after orienting yourself. Follow a safe route rather than the shortest route. Do not descend cliffs, enter floodwater, or follow an unknown river through dangerous terrain.

Use landmarks, sun position with caution, terrain shape, and a written direction log. Do not rely on improvised claims about moss, stars, or animal behavior as a sole direction method.

## 8. Sanitation and disease prevention

Separate drinking water, washing water, and waste areas. Keep latrines downhill and away from water sources. Cover waste and wash hands after using the toilet, before cooking, and before eating. Control flies and rodents. Isolate people with vomiting, diarrhea, fever, or respiratory illness when practical and seek medical advice.

Do not burn plastics, batteries, treated wood, or unknown chemicals in a cooking fire. Avoid breathing smoke. Handle dead animals with barriers and wash afterward.

## 9. Practical repair kit

A compact kit can include cordage, repair tape, sewing needles and thread, work gloves, pliers, a multi-bit driver, a small saw, a headlamp, spare batteries, zip ties, wire, patches, containers, water-treatment supplies, and a marker. Protect cutting edges. Carry tools legally and use eye protection.

Safe field projects include repairing a shelter, making a drying line, improving a water container, building a raised sleeping platform, creating a signal flag, organizing a pack, and making a simple splint under medical guidance. Do not make weapons, explosives, harmful traps, or devices intended to injure people.

## 10. Communication and community

Use plain language and short messages: location, hazard, number of people, injuries, supplies, and requested help. Keep a written roster. Agree on meeting points and check-in times. Share verified information, not rumors. Do not publish the location of vulnerable people or supplies.

Cooperate with neighbors, trained responders, shelters, and local authorities. De-escalate conflict, create distance, leave when safe, and seek help. Survival is more reliable when people coordinate.

## 11. Public-domain field books included offline

The `library/` folder includes complete Project Gutenberg text editions of **Woodcraft and Camping** by George Washington Sears (“Nessmuk”) and **Camp-Lore and Woodcraft** by Daniel Carter Beard. Their historical advice is useful for understanding camp organization, clothing, packs, shelter, fires, cooking, knots, and outdoor observation, but some details are dated. Apply modern fire, health, environmental, accessibility, and legal standards first.

The historical books contain hunting and weapon references. This app’s assistant does not turn those passages into instructions for harming people. Use the books for non-violent campcraft, repair, navigation, cooking, shelter, and observation.

## 12. Pack checklist

Water container; treatment method; food; first-aid kit; essential medicines; insulation; rain and sun protection; light; spare power; paper map; compass; whistle; cordage; repair tools; gloves; hygiene supplies; copies of identification and emergency contacts; cash where appropriate; and a written plan.

## Safety boundary

This library is educational preparedness content. It does not replace professional medical, fire, engineering, legal, or emergency advice. When official guidance conflicts with historical camp advice, follow current official guidance.
